# 1.2.1 Pendulum Equation

## Purpose

The pendulum is the canonical nonlinear mechanical system.

It demonstrates

- nonlinear dynamics
    
- multiple equilibria
    
- conservative systems
    
- controlled systems
    

Many aerospace and robotics systems have equations that locally resemble the pendulum.

---

## State Variables

Choose

$$  
x_1=\theta  
$$

$$  
x_2=\dot{\theta}  
$$

where

- $x_1$ = angle
    
- $x_2$ = angular velocity
    

---

## State Equations

$$  
\dot{x}_1=x_2  
$$

$$  
\dot{x}_2=  
-\frac{g}{l}\sin(x_1)  
-\frac{k}{m}x_2  
$$

where

- $g$ = gravity
    
- $l$ = pendulum length
    
- $m$ = mass
    
- $k$ = damping coefficient
    

---

## Why These States?

Position alone does not determine future motion.

Velocity alone does not determine future motion.

Both are required.

---

## Equilibrium Points

Solve

$$  
\dot{x}=0  
$$

Result

$$  
(\theta,\dot{\theta})=(n\pi,0)  
$$

for

$$  
n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\ldots  
$$

Physically, there are only two unique configurations:

- Downward equilibrium
    
- Upright equilibrium
    

The remaining solutions differ by complete revolutions.

---

## Stability

Downward equilibrium

- Stable
    

Upright equilibrium

- Unstable
    

Same equations.

Different stability.

---

## Conservative Pendulum

If

$$  
k=0  
$$

there is no energy loss.

The pendulum continually exchanges

- kinetic energy
    
- potential energy
    

and oscillates forever.

---

## Controlled Pendulum

Applying an external torque gives

$$  
\dot{x}=f(x)+g(x)u  
$$

This is the standard **control-affine form** used throughout nonlinear control.

---

# 1.2.3 Mass-Spring System

## Big Picture

The mass-spring system is a mechanical example of a nonlinear second-order system.

It has the general form

$$
m\ddot{y} = F - F_f - F_{sp}
$$

where

- $m$ = mass
- $y$ = displacement
- $\dot{y}$ = velocity
- $\ddot{y}$ = acceleration
- $F$ = externally applied force
- $F_f$ = friction force
- $F_{sp}$ = spring restoring force

The system becomes nonlinear when either the spring force, friction force, or external force is nonlinear.

---

## State Variables

Choose

$$
x_1 = y
$$

$$
x_2 = \dot{y}
$$

Then

$$
\dot{x}_1 = x_2
$$

and

$$
\dot{x}_2 = \ddot{y}
$$

So the goal is to solve Newton's law for $\ddot{y}$.

---

## General Equation of Motion

Newton's second law gives

$$
m\ddot{y} = F - F_f - F_{sp}
$$

or

$$
\ddot{y}
=
\frac{1}{m}
\left[
F - F_f - F_{sp}
\right]
$$

Therefore the state model is

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2=
\frac{1}{m}
\left[
F - F_f - F_{sp}
\right]
$$

This is already in nonlinear state-space form.

---

## Spring Force

The spring restoring force is written as

$$
F_{sp}=g(y)
$$

where $g(y)$ describes how the spring force depends on displacement.

Khalil assumes the reference position is chosen so that

$$
g(0)=0
$$

meaning there is no spring force at the reference position.

---

## Linear Spring

For small displacement, the spring force is often modeled as

$$
g(y)=ky
$$

where $k$ is the spring constant.

Then the restoring force grows proportionally with displacement.

If displacement doubles, spring force doubles.

This is the standard Hooke's law model.

$$
F_{sp}=ky
$$

The equation becomes

$$
m\ddot{y}=F-F_f-ky
$$

This is linear if $F_f$ is also linear.

---

## Softening Spring

A softening spring becomes less stiff as displacement grows.

Khalil gives the example

$$
g(y)=k(1-a^2y^2)y
$$

or equivalently

$$
g(y)=ky-ka^2y^3
$$

The linear part is

$$
ky
$$

The nonlinear correction is

$$
-ka^2y^3
$$

Because the cubic term subtracts from the linear term, the effective stiffness decreases as $|y|$ grows.

---

## Softening Spring Intuition

For small $y$:

$$
a^2y^2 \approx 0
$$

so

$$
g(y)\approx ky
$$

The spring behaves almost linearly.

For larger $y$:

$$
a^2y^2
$$

becomes significant, so

$$
1-a^2y^2
$$

gets smaller.

Therefore the spring force grows more slowly than a linear spring.

Physically:

- small displacement: normal spring behavior
- large displacement: spring becomes easier to stretch
- restoring force does not increase as aggressively

---

## Softening Spring Shape

Linear spring:

$$
g(y)=ky
$$

Softening spring:

$$
g(y)=ky-ka^2y^3
$$

For positive $y$, the cubic term bends the force curve downward.

That means the force-displacement curve has a decreasing slope as displacement grows.

The slope of the restoring force is the effective stiffness:

$$
k_{\text{eff}}(y)=\frac{dg}{dy}
$$

For the softening spring,

$$
k_{\text{eff}}(y)=k-3ka^2y^2
$$

So stiffness decreases as $|y|$ increases.

---

## Hardening Spring

A hardening spring becomes stiffer as displacement grows.

Khalil gives the example

$$
g(y)=k(1+a^2y^2)y
$$

or

$$
g(y)=ky+ka^2y^3
$$

Now the cubic term adds to the linear force.

---

## Hardening Spring Intuition

For small $y$:

$$
g(y)\approx ky
$$

For larger $y$:

$$
ka^2y^3
$$

becomes important.

The restoring force grows faster than a linear spring.

Physically:

- small displacement: normal spring behavior
- large displacement: spring becomes harder to stretch
- restoring force increases aggressively

---

## Hardening Spring Shape

Linear spring:

$$
g(y)=ky
$$

Hardening spring:

$$
g(y)=ky+ka^2y^3
$$

The force-displacement curve bends upward.

The effective stiffness is

$$
k_{\text{eff}}(y)=k+3ka^2y^2
$$

So stiffness increases as $|y|$ increases.

---

## Comparing Spring Modes

| Spring Type | Force Law | Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | $g(y)=ky$ | Constant stiffness |
| Softening | $g(y)=ky-ka^2y^3$ | Stiffness decreases with displacement |
| Hardening | $g(y)=ky+ka^2y^3$ | Stiffness increases with displacement |

---

## Why This Matters

Linear spring systems have one natural frequency:

$$
\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

But nonlinear springs do not have a single fixed natural frequency.

The oscillation frequency can depend on amplitude.

For a hardening spring:

- larger oscillations tend to have higher effective stiffness
- frequency can increase with amplitude

For a softening spring:

- larger oscillations tend to have lower effective stiffness
- frequency can decrease with amplitude

This is one of the key ways nonlinear oscillators differ from linear oscillators.

---

## Friction Models

Khalil also discusses several friction effects.

## Viscous Friction

Viscous friction is proportional to velocity:

$$
F_f=c\dot{y}
$$

or

$$
F_f=cx_2
$$

This is the simplest damping model.

It is linear.

---

## Coulomb Friction

Coulomb friction has approximately constant magnitude and always opposes motion.

$$
F_f=\mu_k mg\, \text{sign}(\dot{y})
$$

This is nonlinear because of the sign function.

The force jumps depending on the direction of motion.

---

## Static Friction

Static friction acts when the mass is not moving.

It adjusts its value to prevent motion up to some maximum limit.

That means the friction force is not simply a smooth function of velocity.

This can create discontinuities in the model.

---

## Stribeck Effect

The Stribeck effect describes friction that decreases as sliding velocity increases from zero.

This captures the transition from static friction to kinetic friction more realistically.

It is important in systems with stick-slip behavior.

---

## Duffing Equation

A famous nonlinear oscillator appears when the system has

- hardening spring
- linear viscous damping
- periodic forcing

Khalil writes this as Duffing's equation:

$$
m\ddot{y}+c\dot{y}+ky+ka^2y^3=A\cos(\omega t)
$$

This is a classic nonlinear second-order system.

It can exhibit behavior that linear mass-spring systems cannot, such as amplitude-dependent frequency response and jump phenomena.

---

## State-Space Form of Duffing Equation

Let

$$
x_1=y
$$

$$
x_2=\dot{y}
$$

Then

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2
$$

and

$$
\dot{x}_2=
-\frac{c}{m}x_2
-\frac{k}{m}x_1
-\frac{ka^2}{m}x_1^3
+\frac{A}{m}\cos(\omega t)
$$

This system is nonlinear because of the cubic term

$$
x_1^3
$$

and nonautonomous because of the explicit time-dependent forcing

$$
\cos(\omega t)
$$

---

## Engineering Interpretation

The mass-spring system is useful because it shows how nonlinearities can enter through ordinary physical effects:

- nonlinear stiffness
- nonlinear damping
- static friction
- Coulomb friction
- periodic forcing

In aerospace, similar nonlinearities appear in

- structural vibration
- flexible wings
- landing gear
- actuator friction
- control surface hinge moments
- aeroelasticity
- slosh dynamics

---

## Key Takeaways

- The mass-spring system starts from Newton's law.
- The states are displacement and velocity.
- A linear spring has constant stiffness.
- A softening spring becomes less stiff at large displacement.
- A hardening spring becomes more stiff at large displacement.
- Nonlinear springs can make frequency depend on amplitude.
- Friction can introduce discontinuities and piecewise behavior.
- Duffing's equation is a classic nonlinear oscillator.

# 1.2.4 Negative-Resistance Oscillator

## Circuit Components

## Big Picture

The negative-resistance oscillator is an electrical circuit that can produce sustained oscillations.

It is important because it leads to two classic nonlinear oscillator equations:

$$
\ddot{v}+f(v)\dot{v}+g(v)=0
$$

called **Liénard's equation**, and a special case called the **Van der Pol equation**.

The major physical idea is:

> The circuit contains an active nonlinear element that can add energy for small signals and remove energy for large signals.

That balance allows stable oscillation.

---

## Circuit Variables

The circuit contains:

- capacitor $C$
- inductor $L$
- nonlinear resistive element
- capacitor voltage $v$
- capacitor current $i_C$
- inductor current $i_L$
- nonlinear element current $i$

The nonlinear element is described by

$$
i=h(v)
$$

where $h(v)$ is the current through the active element as a function of voltage.

---

## What Does Negative Resistance Mean?

For an ordinary resistor,

$$
i=\frac{v}{R}
$$

so the slope of the current-voltage curve is positive:

$$
\frac{di}{dv}>0
$$

That means increasing voltage increases current.

For a negative-resistance device, near the origin,

$$
h'(0)<0
$$

so increasing voltage can decrease current.

This is not a passive resistor.

It is an active device that can inject energy into the circuit.

---

## Conditions on the Nonlinear Element

Khalil assumes

$$
h(0)=0
$$

meaning zero voltage produces zero current.

He also assumes

$$
h'(0)<0
$$

meaning the element has negative incremental resistance near the origin.

He further assumes

$$
h(v)\to \infty
\quad \text{as} \quad
v\to \infty
$$

and

$$
h(v)\to -\infty
\quad \text{as} \quad
v\to -\infty
$$

This means the nonlinearity eventually grows large enough to limit the oscillation.

---

## Physical Interpretation

Near the origin:

$$
h'(0)<0
$$

The circuit behaves as if it has negative damping.

Small oscillations grow.

Far from the origin:

$$
h(v)
$$

eventually becomes large and opposes further growth.

Large oscillations are limited.

So the system naturally settles into a finite-amplitude oscillation.

This is the basic mechanism behind a stable nonlinear oscillator.

---

## Step 1: Apply Kirchhoff's Current Law

At the main node, the currents are

- capacitor current $i_C$
- inductor current $i_L$
- nonlinear element current $i=h(v)$

Using KCL:

$$
i_C+i_L+i=0
$$

Substitute the nonlinear element current:

$$
i_C+i_L+h(v)=0
$$

---

## Step 2: Use the Capacitor Equation

For a capacitor,

$$
i_C=C\frac{dv}{dt}
$$

or

$$
i_C=C\dot{v}
$$

Substitute into KCL:

$$
C\dot{v}+i_L+h(v)=0
$$

This relates the capacitor voltage rate to the inductor current and nonlinear element current.

---

## Step 3: Express the Inductor Current

For an inductor,

$$
v=L\frac{di_L}{dt}
$$

so

$$
\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{v}{L}
$$

To express $i_L$ in terms of $v$, integrate both sides:
### This differential equation is a bit weird

Starting with the differential equation

$$
\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{v(t)}{L},
$$

we integrate both sides from an initial time \(t_0\) to the current time \(t\):

$$
\int_{t_0}^{t}\frac{di_L}{dt}\,dt
=
\frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t}v(s)\,ds.
$$

---

#### Evaluating the Left-Hand Side

The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** states that

$$
\int_{a}^{b}\frac{df}{dt}\,dt
=
f(b)-f(a).
$$

Applying this to the inductor current,

$$
\int_{t_0}^{t}\frac{di_L}{dt}\,dt
=
i_L(t)-i_L(t_0).
$$

Substituting this result gives

$$
i_L(t)-i_L(t_0)
=
\frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t}v(s)\,ds.
$$

Finally, solve for the current:

$$
i_L(t)
=
i_L(t_0)
+
\frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t}v(s)\,ds.
$$

---

##### Special Case Used by Khalil

Khalil chooses

$$
t_0=-\infty,
$$

and assumes the circuit has been at rest for an infinitely long time:

$$
i_L(-\infty)=0.
$$

Substituting this into the general solution gives

$$
i_L(t)
=
\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{t}v(s)\,ds,
$$
---

## Step 4: Differentiate the Equation (to remove the integral)

Start with

$$
C\dot{v}
+
\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{t}v(s)\,ds
+
h(v)
=
0
$$

Differentiate term by term.

First term:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(C\dot{v}\right)
=
C\ddot{v}
$$

Second term:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\left[
\frac{1}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{t}v(s)\,ds
\right]
=
\frac{1}{L}v
$$

Third term:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}h(v)
=
h'(v)\dot{v}
$$

So the differentiated equation is

$$
C\ddot{v}
+
\frac{1}{L}v
+
h'(v)\dot{v}
=
0
$$

---

## Step 5: Multiply by \(L\)

Multiply the entire equation by $L$:

$$
LC\ddot{v}
+
v
+
Lh'(v)\dot{v}
=
0
$$

Rearrange:

$$
LC\ddot{v}
+
Lh'(v)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

This is the circuit equation before nondimensionalizing time.

---

## Step 6: Change the Time Variable

Khalil changes time from $t$ to a scaled time variable

$$
\tau=\frac{t}{\sqrt{LC}}
$$

This removes the $LC$ coefficient and makes the oscillator equation cleaner.

The derivatives transform as

$$
\frac{dv}{dt}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\frac{dv}{d\tau}
$$

and

$$
\frac{d^2v}{dt^2}
=
\frac{1}{LC}\frac{d^2v}{d\tau^2}
$$

Using dots now to mean derivatives with respect to $\tau$, the equation becomes

$$
\ddot{v}
+
\epsilon h'(v)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

where

$$
\epsilon=\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}
$$

---

## Liénard Form

The equation

$$
\ddot{v}
+
\epsilon h'(v)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

is a special case of Liénard's equation:

$$
\ddot{v}+f(v)\dot{v}+g(v)=0
$$

For this circuit,

$$
f(v)=\epsilon h'(v)
$$

and

$$
g(v)=v
$$

---

## Van der Pol Oscillator

Khalil then chooses a specific nonlinear characteristic:

$$
h(v)=-v+\frac{1}{3}v^3
$$

Differentiate:

$$
h'(v)=-1+v^2
$$

Substitute into the oscillator equation:

$$
\ddot{v}
+
\epsilon(-1+v^2)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

Rewrite:

$$
\ddot{v}
-
\epsilon(1-v^2)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

This is the **Van der Pol equation**:

$$
\ddot{v}
-
\epsilon(1-v^2)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

---

## Why the Van der Pol Equation Oscillates

Look at the damping-like term:

$$
-\epsilon(1-v^2)\dot{v}
$$

For small $v$:

$$
v^2<1
$$

so

$$
1-v^2>0
$$

and the damping term behaves like negative damping.

That adds energy.

Small oscillations grow.

For large $v$:

$$
v^2>1
$$

so

$$
1-v^2<0
$$

and the damping becomes positive.

That removes energy.

Large oscillations shrink.

Therefore the system naturally settles into a stable oscillation.

This is called a **limit cycle**.

---

## State-Space Model 1

Choose states

$$
x_1=v
$$

$$
x_2=\dot{v}
$$

Then

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2
$$

From

$$
\ddot{v}
+
\epsilon h'(v)\dot{v}
+
v
=
0
$$

solve for $\ddot{v}$:

$$
\ddot{v}
=
-v-\epsilon h'(v)\dot{v}
$$

Substitute the states:

$$
\dot{x}_2
=
-x_1-\epsilon h'(x_1)x_2
$$

So the state model is

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2=-x_1-\epsilon h'(x_1)x_2
$$

---

## State-Space Model 2: Physical Circuit States

Khalil also notes that we can choose the physical electrical states instead.

Let

$$
z_1=i_L
$$

$$
z_2=v_C
$$

where

- $z_1$ = inductor current
- $z_2$ = capacitor voltage

From the inductor equation,

$$
v=L\frac{di_L}{dt}
$$

so

$$
\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{v}{L}
$$

Using $z_2=v$:

$$
\dot{z}_1=\frac{1}{L}z_2
$$

Depending on sign convention, Khalil's chosen current direction gives

$$
\dot{z}_1=-\frac{1}{L}z_2
$$

The sign comes from the orientation of the current arrow and voltage polarity in the circuit diagram.

---

## Capacitor Equation for \(z_2\)

From KCL:

$$
i_C+i_L+h(v)=0
$$

Use

$$
i_C=C\dot{v}
$$

and

$$
v=z_2
$$

Then

$$
C\dot{z}_2+z_1+h(z_2)=0
$$

Solve for $\dot{z}_2$:

$$
\dot{z}_2
=
-\frac{1}{C}
\left[
z_1+h(z_2)
\right]
$$

So the physical-state model is essentially

$$
\dot{z}_1=-\frac{1}{L}z_2
$$

$$
\dot{z}_2=
-\frac{1}{C}
\left[
z_1+h(z_2)
\right]
$$

Khalil then rescales time to match the previous nondimensional form.

---

## Why Two State Models Are Allowed

The two models use different states:

First model:

$$
x_1=v
$$

$$
x_2=\dot{v}
$$

Second model:

$$
z_1=i_L
$$

$$
z_2=v_C
$$

They look different, but they describe the same physical circuit.

This is a major point:

> A state-space model is not unique.

Different valid choices of state variables can describe the same system.

They are related by a change of coordinates.

---

## Coordinate Transformation

The two state descriptions are related by

$$
z=T(x)
$$

and

$$
x=T^{-1}(z)
$$

This means we can transform from one state representation to another without changing the underlying system.

This idea becomes very important later in nonlinear control, especially in feedback linearization.

---

## Engineering Interpretation

The negative-resistance oscillator is important because it shows how a system can create stable oscillations without external periodic forcing.

The energy mechanism is:

- small amplitude: active element injects energy
- large amplitude: nonlinear element dissipates energy
- final result: stable self-sustained oscillation

This is very different from a linear LC circuit, where oscillations depend on initial energy and are destroyed by resistance.

---

## Connection to Nonlinear Systems Theory

This example introduces several major ideas:

- nonlinear damping

- limit cycles
- Liénard systems

- pilot-induced oscillations

In these systems, energy may be added at small amplitudes and removed at large amplitudes, producing finite-amplitude oscillations.

h(v)=-v+\frac{1}{3}v^3
$$

the system becomes the Van der Pol oscillator.
- The Van der Pol oscillator has negative damping for small amplitudes and positive damping for large amplitudes.
- This creates a stable limit cycle.
- The same physical system can have multiple valid state-space representations.
