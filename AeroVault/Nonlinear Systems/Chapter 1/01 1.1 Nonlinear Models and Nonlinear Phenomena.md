
## Big Picture

Linear control is built around

$$  
\dot{x} = Ax + Bu  
$$

where the system behaves predictably because of the **superposition principle**.

---
Linear systems satisfy the **superposition principle**, which is made up of two properties:  
  
## Additivity  
  
If two inputs produce two individual responses,  
  
$$  
u_1 \rightarrow x_1  
$$  
  
and  
  
$$  
u_2 \rightarrow x_2,  
$$  
  
then applying both inputs simultaneously produces  
  
$$  
u_1 + u_2 \rightarrow x_1 + x_2.  
$$  
  
The responses simply add together.  
  
---  
  
## Homogeneity (Scaling)  
  
If an input  
  
$$  
u  
$$  
  
produces a response  
  
$$  
x,  
$$  
  
then scaling the input by a constant $a$ produces  
  
$$  
au \rightarrow ax.  
$$  
  
The output scales by exactly the same amount as the input.  
  
---  
  
## Together  
  
When **additivity** and **homogeneity** are both true, the system satisfies  
  
$$  
L(au_1 + bu_2)  
=  
aL(u_1)+bL(u_2),  
$$  
  
where $L(\cdot)$ represents the system.  
  
This property is what allows us to use powerful linear analysis tools such as  
  
- Eigenvalues  
- Eigenvectors  
- Matrix exponentials  
- Transfer functions  
- Laplace transforms  
- Frequency response  
- Fourier analysis  
  
---  
  
# Why Doesn't It Work for Nonlinear Systems?  
  
A nonlinear system contains terms that prevent outputs from combining linearly.  
  
Examples include  
  
$$  
x^2,  
$$  
  
$$  
\sin(x),  
$$  
  
$$  
e^x,  
$$  
  
$$  
x_1x_2,  
$$  
  
or any other nonlinear function.  
  
Suppose  
  
$$  
f(x)=x^2.  
$$  
  
Then  
  
$$  
f(x_1+x_2)  
=  
(x_1+x_2)^2  
=  
x_1^2+2x_1x_2+x_2^2.  
$$  
  
Notice  
  
$$  
f(x_1+x_2)  
\neq  
f(x_1)+f(x_2),  
$$  
  
because of the extra interaction term  
  
$$  
2x_1x_2.  
$$  
  
The responses no longer simply add together.  
  
Likewise,  
  
$$  
f(ax)  
=  
(ax)^2  
=  
a^2x^2  
\neq  
af(x).  
$$  
  
Scaling the input no longer scales the output proportionally.  
  
Therefore, **both additivity and homogeneity fail**, and the superposition principle no longer applies.

# State-Space Model

Every nonlinear system can be written as

$$  
\dot{x}=f(t,x,u)  
$$

where

- $x$ = state vector
    
- $u$ = input vector
    
- $f$ = nonlinear dynamics
    

Sometimes an output equation is also defined

$$  
y=h(t,x,u)  
$$

---

## State

The **state** contains the minimum information required to uniquely determine the future motion of the system.

Think of the state as the system's **memory**.

Example:

Pendulum

Need

- angle
    
- angular velocity
    

Knowing only one is insufficient.

---

## Input

The input is an external quantity that drives or controls the system.

Examples

- motor torque
    
- elevator deflection
    
- rudder angle
    
- voltage
    
- thrust
    

---

## Output

The output is what we observe or measure.

Examples

- position
    
- velocity
    
- attitude
    
- temperature
    

The output does **not** have to equal the state.

---

# Why First-Order Equations?

Any finite-order differential equation can be rewritten as a system of coupled first-order equations.

Example

Original equation

$$  
\ddot{x}+2\dot{x}+5x=u  
$$

Define

$$  
x_1=x  
$$

$$  
x_2=\dot{x}  
$$

Then

$$  
\dot{x}_1=x_2  
$$

$$  
\dot{x}_2=-5x_1-2x_2+u  
$$

Every nonlinear control method in this book assumes this first-order state-space form.

---

# Autonomous vs Nonautonomous Systems

## Autonomous

$$  
\dot{x}=f(x)  
$$

No explicit dependence on time.

Examples

- Pendulum
    
- Satellite attitude
    
- Robot arm
    

---

## Nonautonomous

$$  
\dot{x}=f(t,x)  
$$

The dynamics explicitly depend on time.

Examples

- Time-varying references
    
- Scheduled controllers
    
- Environmental disturbances
    

---

# Equilibrium Point

An equilibrium is a state that never changes.

Mathematically

$$  
\dot{x}=0  
$$

For an autonomous system

$$  
f(x^*)=0  
$$

Interpretation:

If the system starts exactly at an equilibrium, it remains there forever.

---

# Why Linearization Isn't Enough

Linearization approximates a nonlinear system near one operating point.

It tells us what happens **locally**, but cannot predict

- global behavior
    
- multiple equilibria
    
- nonlinear oscillations
    
- chaos
    

---

# Essentially Nonlinear Phenomena

These cannot occur in purely linear systems.

## Finite Escape Time

The state reaches infinity in finite time.

Linear systems only diverge asymptotically.

---

## Multiple Equilibria

A nonlinear system may possess several equilibrium points.

The final equilibrium depends on the initial condition.

---

## Limit Cycles

Stable self-sustained oscillations.

Examples

- Pendulum clock
    
- Engine idle
    
- Aircraft flutter
    
- Heartbeat
    

---

## Harmonics and Subharmonics

A periodic input may generate

- higher harmonics
    
- lower harmonics
    

This cannot happen in linear systems.

---

## Chaos

A deterministic nonlinear system can exhibit apparently random motion.

Small differences in initial conditions grow dramatically over time.

---

## Multiple Modes of Behavior

The same nonlinear system may

- converge
    
- oscillate
    
- diverge
    

depending solely on its initial condition.

---

# Key Takeaways

- The state is the system's memory.
    
- Every finite-dimensional system can be written in state-space form.
    
- Equilibria satisfy $\dot{x}=0$.
    
- Linearization is only a local approximation.
    
- Nonlinear systems exhibit behaviors impossible for linear systems.
