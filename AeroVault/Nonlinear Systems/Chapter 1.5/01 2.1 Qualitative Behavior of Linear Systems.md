# Chapter 2 - Second-Order Systems

Chapter 2 introduces the qualitative analysis of nonlinear systems using **second-order differential equations**. Unlike linear systems, nonlinear systems often cannot be solved analytically. Instead of focusing on explicit solutions $x(t)$, we study how the **state evolves in the phase plane**.

The key idea is that every point in the state space has an associated direction of motion. By studying these trajectories, we can determine stability, equilibrium points, oscillations, and other important system behaviors without solving the differential equations directly.

Most concepts introduced in this chapter become the foundation for later topics including:

- Phase portraits
- Equilibrium points
- Stability
- Limit cycles
- Lyapunov methods
- Nonlinear control design

---

# 2.1 Phase Plane Analysis

A second-order autonomous nonlinear system can be written as two coupled first-order differential equations

$$
\dot{x}_1=f_1(x_1,x_2)
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2=f_2(x_1,x_2)
$$

where

- $x_1$ is the first state variable
- $x_2$ is the second state variable
- $f_1(\cdot)$ and $f_2(\cdot)$ are generally nonlinear functions of the state

The system state is

$$
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since the system is **autonomous**, neither equation depends explicitly on time.

Instead, the current state completely determines the direction of motion.

---

## Interpretation

Each point

$$
(x_1,x_2)
$$

represents one possible state of the system.

At every point, the differential equations determine

$$
\dot{x}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{x}_1\\
\dot{x}_2
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_1(x_1,x_2)\\
f_2(x_1,x_2)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This vector tells us the instantaneous direction in which the state will move.

By evaluating this direction at many points, we can sketch the **phase portrait**, which shows the qualitative behavior of the system without explicitly solving the differential equations.

---
## Aside: The Isocline Method

Before computers could easily simulate nonlinear systems, phase portraits were often constructed using the **isocline method**.

An **isocline** is a curve in the phase plane along which the trajectory has the same slope.

Starting with

$$
\dot{x}_1=f_1(x_1,x_2)
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2=f_2(x_1,x_2),
$$

the slope of a trajectory is

$$
\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}
=
\frac{\dot{x}_2}{\dot{x}_1}
=
\frac{f_2(x_1,x_2)}{f_1(x_1,x_2)}.
$$

Notice that **time disappears** from this equation.

Instead of solving for how the state evolves with time, we determine the direction of motion at every point in the phase plane.

### Constructing a Phase Portrait

The basic procedure is:

1. Compute the trajectory slope

   $$
   \frac{dx_2}{dx_1}
   =
   \frac{f_2}{f_1}.
   $$

2. Choose several slope values (e.g. $0$, $\pm1$, $\infty$).

3. Solve for the curves where each slope occurs. These curves are the **isoclines**.

4. Draw small line segments having the appropriate slope along each isocline.

5. Sketch trajectories that smoothly follow these line segments while respecting the direction given by the vector field.

Although modern software constructs phase portraits numerically, the isocline method provides valuable intuition for why trajectories take their observed shapes.

### Example: Pendulum Without Friction

The nonlinear pendulum is described by

$$
\ddot{\theta}
+
\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta
=
0.
$$

Define the states

$$
x_1=\theta,
$$

$$
x_2=\dot{\theta},
$$

giving

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2,
$$

$$
\dot{x}_2
=
-\frac{g}{L}\sin x_1.
$$

The trajectory slope becomes

$$
\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}
=
-\frac{g}{L}
\frac{\sin x_1}{x_2}.
$$

Several important isoclines immediately appear:

### Horizontal tangents

A horizontal tangent occurs when

$$
\frac{dx_2}{dx_1}=0,
$$

which requires

$$
\dot{x}_2=0.
$$

Since

$$
\dot{x}_2
=
-\frac{g}{L}\sin x_1,
$$

the horizontal isoclines are

$$
x_1=n\pi,
\qquad
n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\ldots
$$

These correspond to the pendulum being exactly upright or hanging straight down.

---

### Vertical tangents

Vertical tangents occur whenever

$$
\dot{x}_1=0.
$$

Since

$$
\dot{x}_1=x_2,
$$

the vertical isocline is simply

$$
x_2=0.
$$

This is the line where the pendulum momentarily stops before reversing direction.

---

### Physical Interpretation

Near the stable equilibrium

$$
(\theta,\dot{\theta})=(0,0),
$$

the isoclines combine to form closed trajectories.

Each closed curve represents a pendulum oscillating with a fixed total energy.

Unlike a damped pendulum, there is no friction to remove energy, so the trajectories never spiral inward. Instead, they remain closed loops surrounding the equilibrium.

The isocline method makes these closed orbits understandable without explicitly solving the nonlinear differential equation.

## Qualitative Behavior of Linear Systems

Before studying nonlinear systems, Khalil reviews the qualitative behavior of **linear time-invariant (LTI)** systems. The goal is not to solve the differential equation—we already know how—but to understand how the **eigenvalues determine the geometry of trajectories in the phase plane.**

Consider the linear system

$$
\dot{x}=Ax,
$$

where

- $x\in\mathbb{R}^2$ is the state vector
- $A$ is a constant $2\times2$ matrix

For a given initial condition $x_0$,

$$
x(0)=x_0,
$$

the solution is

$$
x(t)=e^{At}x_0.
$$

Rather than computing the matrix exponential directly, Khalil transforms the system into a simpler coordinate system.

---

## Why Transform the System?

Every real matrix can be transformed into a simpler equivalent form.

Define a new coordinate system

$$
z=M^{-1}x,
$$

where $M$ is an invertible matrix.

Substituting,

$$
x=Mz,
$$

into

$$
\dot{x}=Ax
$$

gives

$$
M\dot{z}=AMz.
$$

Multiplying by $M^{-1}$,

$$
\dot{z}
=
M^{-1}AMz.
$$

The matrix

$$
J_r=M^{-1}AM
$$

is called the **real Jordan form** of $A$.

Instead of studying

$$
\dot{x}=Ax,
$$

we study the equivalent system

$$
\dot{z}=J_rz,
$$

which has exactly the same qualitative behavior but is much easier to analyze.

---

## Solution in Jordan Coordinates

Since the transformed system is simpler,

$$
z(t)
=
e^{J_rt}z_0.
$$

Transforming back to the original coordinates,

$$
x(t)
=
Me^{J_rt}M^{-1}x_0.
$$

The matrices $M$ and $M^{-1}$ simply rotate and scale the coordinate system.

**Important:** They do **not** change the stability or overall shape of the trajectories.

The qualitative behavior depends entirely on the eigenvalues contained in $J_r$.

---

## The Three Possible Jordan Forms

A real $2\times2$ matrix has only three possible qualitative forms.

### Case 1: Two Distinct Real Eigenvalues

$$
J_r=
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_1 & 0\\
0 & \lambda_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This occurs when

$$
\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2.
$$

Each state evolves independently,

$$
z_1(t)=e^{\lambda_1t}z_1(0)
$$

$$
z_2(t)=e^{\lambda_2t}z_2(0).
$$

The signs of the eigenvalues determine whether trajectories move toward or away from the origin.

---

### Case 2: Repeated Real Eigenvalue

$$
J_r=
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda & k\\
0 & \lambda
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where

$$
k=
\begin{cases}
0,&\text{matrix is diagonalizable}\\
1,&\text{matrix is defective}
\end{cases}
$$

If the matrix has only one independent eigenvector, the off-diagonal term cannot be removed.

This creates the familiar **improper node** seen in phase portraits.

---

### Case 3: Complex Conjugate Eigenvalues

If

$$
\lambda_{1,2}
=
\alpha\pm j\beta,
$$

the Jordan form becomes

$$
J_r=
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha & -\beta\\
\beta & \alpha
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Notice this matrix contains no complex numbers even though the eigenvalues are complex.

The parameter

- $\alpha$ controls exponential growth or decay.
- $\beta$ controls rotation.

If

$$
\alpha<0,
$$

the trajectories spiral inward.

If

$$
\alpha>0,
$$

they spiral outward.

If

$$
\alpha=0,
$$

the trajectories become closed orbits (centers).

---

## Why Khalil Uses the Jordan Form

In an introductory controls course, we usually compute the eigenvalues directly and classify the system.

Khalil instead begins with the Jordan form because:

- it provides one mathematical framework for every possible $2\times2$ linear system,
- it makes the geometry of the trajectories easier to derive,
- it extends naturally to higher-dimensional systems later in the book.

The key takeaway is that **every linear second-order system behaves like one of these three canonical forms after an appropriate change of coordinates.**

# Phase Plane Cases

Khalil begins with several special cases where the trajectories can be determined almost entirely by inspection. These build intuition before analyzing more general nonlinear systems.

## Case 1: Distinct Real Eigenvalues

Assume the system

$$
\dot{x}=Ax
$$

has two distinct real, nonzero eigenvalues

$$
\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2.
$$

Since the eigenvalues are distinct, there are two linearly independent eigenvectors

$$
v_1,\;v_2.
$$

These eigenvectors define a new coordinate system.

---

### Change of Coordinates

Construct the matrix

$$
M=[v_1\;v_2].
$$

The coordinate transformation

$$
z=M^{-1}x
$$

expresses the state in the eigenvector basis.

In these new coordinates, the dynamics become

$$
\dot{z}=J_rz,
$$

where

$$
J_r=
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_1&0\\
0&\lambda_2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The important consequence is that the two state equations become **completely decoupled**:

$$
\dot{z}_1=\lambda_1z_1
$$

$$
\dot{z}_2=\lambda_2z_2.
$$

Instead of solving one coupled second-order system, we now solve two independent first-order systems.

---

### Solving the Equations

Each equation has the familiar exponential solution.

For the first coordinate,

$$
\dot{z}_1=\lambda_1z_1
$$

gives

$$
z_1(t)=z_{10}e^{\lambda_1t},
$$

where $z_{10}$ is the initial value.

Likewise,

$$
z_2(t)=z_{20}e^{\lambda_2t}.
$$

Notice that **each coordinate evolves independently at its own exponential rate**.

---

### Eliminating Time

Rather than plotting the solution versus time, we want the **trajectory in the phase plane**.

Solve the first equation for the exponential,

$$
e^{\lambda_1t}
=
\frac{z_1}{z_{10}}.
$$

Raise both sides to the power

$$
\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1},
$$

giving

$$
e^{\lambda_2t}
=
\left(
\frac{z_1}{z_{10}}
\right)^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}.
$$

Substitute this into

$$
z_2=z_{20}e^{\lambda_2t}
$$

to obtain

$$
z_2
=
z_{20}
\left(
\frac{z_1}{z_{10}}
\right)^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}.
$$

Collecting constants,

$$
c
=
\frac{z_{20}}
{z_{10}^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}},
$$

gives the family of trajectories

$$
z_2
=
cz_1^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}.
$$

This equation describes the **shape of every trajectory** in the phase plane.

Notice that **time no longer appears**.

---

### Fast and Slow Eigenvalues

Suppose

$$
\lambda_2<\lambda_1<0.
$$

For example,

$$
\lambda_1=-1,\qquad
\lambda_2=-5.
$$

Both states decay toward zero,

but

$$
e^{-5t}
$$

decays much faster than

$$
e^{-t}.
$$

Therefore,

- $\lambda_2$ is called the **fast eigenvalue**.
- $\lambda_1$ is called the **slow eigenvalue**.

Likewise,

- $v_2$ is the **fast eigenvector**.
- $v_1$ is the **slow eigenvector**.

---

### Why Do Trajectories End Along the Slow Eigenvector?

Initially, both exponential terms contribute.

As time increases,

$$
e^{-5t}
$$

shrinks much faster than

$$
e^{-t}.
$$

Eventually,

the contribution associated with the fast eigenvalue becomes negligible.

The remaining motion is dominated by

$$
e^{-t},
$$

which points in the direction of the slow eigenvector.

This explains an important geometric observation:

> **Trajectories always approach the equilibrium tangent to the slow eigenvector.**

---

### Why Does Khalil Compute the Slope?

Differentiating

$$
z_2
=
cz_1^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}
$$

gives

$$
\frac{dz_2}{dz_1}
=
c
\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}
z_1^{(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)-1}.
$$

This tells us how the trajectory bends as it approaches the origin.

For

$$
\lambda_2<\lambda_1<0,
$$

the exponent satisfies

$$
\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}>1,
$$

so as

$$
z_1\rightarrow0,
$$

the slope approaches zero.

Therefore, the trajectory becomes nearly horizontal near the origin in the $(z_1,z_2)$ coordinates.

Since the horizontal axis is aligned with the slow eigenvector, this mathematically proves that the trajectory becomes tangent to the slow eigenvector.

# Case 2: Complex Eigenvalues

Suppose the matrix $A$ has the complex conjugate eigenvalues

$$
\lambda_{1,2}=\alpha\pm j\beta,
$$

where

- $\alpha$ is the real part.
- $\beta$ is the imaginary part.

Instead of diagonalizing the system with complex coordinates, Khalil transforms it into the equivalent **real Jordan form**

$$
J_r=
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha & -\beta\\
\beta & \alpha
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The transformed state equations become

$$
\dot{z}_1=\alpha z_1-\beta z_2,
$$

$$
\dot{z}_2=\beta z_1+\alpha z_2.
$$

Unlike the previous case, these equations are **coupled**. Each state depends on the other.

---

## Writing the System in Matrix Form

The system can be written as

$$
\dot{z}
=
\left(
\alpha I
+
\beta
\begin{bmatrix}
0&-1\\
1&0
\end{bmatrix}
\right)
z.
$$

Notice that two different matrices appear.

The first,

$$
\alpha I,
$$

simply scales the state.

The second,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
0&-1\\
1&0
\end{bmatrix},
$$

is a pure rotation matrix.

This immediately tells us the qualitative behavior:

- $\alpha$ controls expansion or contraction.
- $\beta$ controls rotation.

---

## Solving the System

The solution is

$$
z(t)=e^{J_rt}z_0.
$$

Since

$$
J_r=\alpha I+\beta R,
$$

where

$$
R=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&-1\\
1&0
\end{bmatrix},
$$

and

$$
IR=RI,
$$

the exponential separates nicely,

$$
e^{J_rt}
=
e^{\alpha t}
e^{\beta Rt}.
$$

The first factor,

$$
e^{\alpha t},
$$

is simply an exponential scaling.

The second factor produces a rotation.

Using the matrix exponential,

$$
e^{\beta Rt}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(\beta t) & -\sin(\beta t)\\
\sin(\beta t) & \cos(\beta t)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
z(t)
=
e^{\alpha t}
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos(\beta t) & -\sin(\beta t)\\
\sin(\beta t) & \cos(\beta t)
\end{bmatrix}
z_0.
$$

---

## Writing the Solution Component-wise

Multiplying by

$$
z_0=
\begin{bmatrix}
z_{10}\\
z_{20}
\end{bmatrix},
$$

gives

$$
z_1(t)
=
e^{\alpha t}
\left(
z_{10}\cos\beta t
-
z_{20}\sin\beta t
\right),
$$

$$
z_2(t)
=
e^{\alpha t}
\left(
z_{10}\sin\beta t
+
z_{20}\cos\beta t
\right).
$$

These equations show two simultaneous effects:

- sinusoidal motion
- exponential scaling

---

# Converting to Polar Coordinates

The geometry becomes much clearer in polar coordinates.

Define

$$
z_1=r\cos\theta,
$$

$$
z_2=r\sin\theta.
$$

The radius is

$$
r=\sqrt{z_1^2+z_2^2}.
$$

Substituting the previous solution,

$$
r(t)
=
r_0e^{\alpha t}.
$$

Therefore,

- if $\alpha<0$, the radius shrinks.
- if $\alpha>0$, the radius grows.
- if $\alpha=0$, the radius stays constant.

Now compute the angle.

Using

$$
\tan\theta
=
\frac{z_2}{z_1},
$$

we obtain

$$
\theta(t)
=
\theta_0+\beta t.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\dot{\theta}
=
\beta.
$$

The angular velocity is constant.

---

# Physical Interpretation

The solution consists of two completely independent motions.

## Radial Motion

The distance from the origin satisfies

$$
r(t)=r_0e^{\alpha t}.
$$

This controls stability.

---

## Angular Motion

The angle satisfies

$$
\theta(t)=\theta_0+\beta t.
$$

This produces continuous rotation around the origin.

---

## Combining Both

Putting both together,

- rotate at constant speed $\beta$
- simultaneously expand or contract according to $\alpha$

This creates spirals.

---

# Three Important Cases

## $\alpha<0$

The radius decreases,

$$
r(t)\rightarrow0.
$$

The trajectory spirals inward.

This is called a **stable focus**.

---

## $\alpha>0$

The radius increases,

$$
r(t)\rightarrow\infty.
$$

The trajectory spirals outward.

This is called an **unstable focus**.

---

## $\alpha=0$

The exponential disappears,

$$
r(t)=r_0.
$$

The radius never changes.

Only rotation remains,

producing closed circular (or elliptical after transforming back to the original coordinates) trajectories.

This is called a **center**.

---

# Connection to Classical Controls

This is exactly the behavior of the familiar second-order poles

$$
s=-\zeta\omega_n
\pm
j\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.
$$

Comparing with

$$
\lambda=\alpha\pm j\beta,
$$

we identify

$$
\alpha=-\zeta\omega_n,
$$

$$
\beta=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}.
$$

Therefore,

- the damping ratio determines how quickly the spiral moves inward,
- the imaginary part determines how quickly the state rotates around the origin.

In a controls course, these parameters describe the oscillatory time response. In phase-plane analysis, they describe the **geometry** of the state trajectory.
